Introduction

Why power matters for ML-based HTE analysis

Machine-learning methods for heterogeneous treatment effect (HTE) estimation in social-science field experiments are becoming increasingly sophisticated. Flexible learners can search across many covariates, nonlinearities, and interactions, but a fitted CATE score is not by itself evidence that treatment effects are truly heterogeneous. Researchers still need an honest inferential test of whether the score captures genuine treatment-effect variation rather than sampling noise.

The generic machine-learning framework of Chernozhukov et al. (2025) evaluates a learned CATE score with the weighted residual best linear predictor (BLP) regression

Y = α0′R(X) + β1{Z − p(X)} + β2{Z − p(X)}S̃A(X) + ε,  w(X) = [p(X){1 − p(X)}]−1.

Here, SA(X) is the CATE score trained in an auxiliary sample and S̃A(X) is its centered version. R(X) contains noise-reducing controls, such as learned baseline and CATE predictions. The population coefficients have direct meanings:

β1 = E{s0(X)} The average treatment effect (ATE), where s0(X) is the true CATE.
β2 = Cov{s0(X), SA(X)} / Var{SA(X)} The heterogeneity loading: the linear alignment between the learned score and the true CATE.

The heterogeneity test is H0: β2 = 0. Rejecting this null in an independent evaluation sample indicates both that HTE exists and that the planned learner has recovered informative variation. Failure to reject can mean either homogeneous effects or a learner that failed to capture existing heterogeneity.

Why the sample must be split

Honest BLP evaluation separates model construction from model assessment. The two samples therefore have different jobs:

Auxiliary sample A

Estimate nuisance functions, tune and train the CATE learner, and fix the score SA(X).

→
Main sample M

Apply the fixed score to independent observations and run the weighted residual BLP test.

Main-sample observations must not be reused to train the score being tested. Repeated splitting can stabilize results across splits, but it does not multiply the effective main-sample size.

The practical power formula

Fu, Green, and Wang (2026) derive a design-stage approximation for the main-sample size needed by a two-sided BLP heterogeneity test:

nM ≈ Γ2α,π₀(1 − RB2) p(1 − p)RS2dτ2 .
α and π0
Two-sided significance level and target power; together they determine Γα,π₀.
p
Treatment-assignment probability. Information is maximized at p = 0.5.
RB2
Out-of-sample predictive R2 of the baseline adjustment; 1 − RB2 is remaining outcome variation.
dτ
Standard deviation of the true CATE in reference outcome-standard-deviation units.
RS2
Fraction of total CATE variation captured by the linear projection on the planned learned score.
nM
Required independent main-sample size, not the experiment's total enrollment.

The approximation assumes constant treatment probability and that unexplained CATE variation and score-weighted heteroskedasticity are modest relative to remaining outcome noise. Use pilot or historical data to inform RB2. Choose dτ as the smallest substantively relevant CATE variation, and treat RS2 as a sensitivity parameter when credible calibration data are unavailable.

Choosing the auxiliary–main share

Allocation Potential benefit Main risk or cost Most relevant when
More to auxiliary A May improve nuisance models and the CATE learner. Leaves fewer observations for honest testing; can require larger total enrollment and may worsen small-main-sample size distortion. HTE is difficult to learn and the procedure needs substantial training data.
More to main M Directly strengthens evaluation, reduces total enrollment needed for a fixed nM, and may improve size control. May weaken the learned score and baseline adjustment by reducing training data. The learner can be trained adequately on a smaller auxiliary sample.

No split is universally optimal. Compare plausible allocations while allowing RB2 and RS2 to change with auxiliary-sample size; do not hold learner quality fixed mechanically across splits.
